# An Evaluation of Long-run Economic Growth: Testing the Solow Growth Model

## Overview

This research paper evaluates the applicability of the **Solow Growth Model** in explaining long-run economic growth, capital accumulation, and productivity across developing and emerging Asian economies from 1970 to 2020.

Using **Bangladesh and Malaysia** as the primary comparative cases, the study examines how physical capital accumulation, human capital, institutional quality, and Total Factor Productivity (TFP) influence differences in income and growth. The paper also tests the **Solow convergence hypothesis** across 11 Asian economies.

The analysis highlights an important distinction between simply accumulating capital and using capital efficiently. It finds that long-run improvements in living standards depend not only on investment but also on productivity growth, human capital, technological progress, and institutional conditions.

## Research Objectives

The paper aims to:

* Examine the theoretical foundations and steady-state conditions of the Solow Growth Model.
* Compare long-run economic growth and capital accumulation in Bangladesh and Malaysia.
* Evaluate changes in capital-output efficiency.
* Calculate and compare decade-level Total Factor Productivity (TFP) growth.
* Examine the relationship between human capital, institutional quality, and productivity.
* Test the convergence hypothesis across 11 Asian economies.

## Theoretical Framework

The study applies the Solow Growth Model using a Cobb-Douglas production function:

$$
Y = A K^{1/3}L^{2/3}
$$

where:

* $Y$ = real output
* $A$ = Total Factor Productivity
* $K$ = physical capital
* $L$ = labour

Capital evolves according to:

$$
\Delta K = sY-\delta K
$$

where $s$ represents the saving/investment rate and $\delta$ represents depreciation.

The economy reaches its steady state when:

$$
sY=\delta K
$$

The model predicts that capital accumulation faces **diminishing returns**, meaning that sustained long-run increases in income per worker increasingly depend on technological progress and productivity growth.

## Comparative Analysis

The comparison between Bangladesh and Malaysia highlights several important differences.

### Capital Accumulation and Efficiency

Malaysia achieved substantially higher levels of real GDP per capita alongside greater capital accumulation and stronger productivity performance.

The study evaluates capital efficiency using changes in capital relative to changes in output:

$$
\frac{\Delta K}{\Delta Y}
$$

This measure is interpreted as an **incremental capital-output ratio**, where a higher value indicates that more additional capital is required to generate an additional unit of output.

### Total Factor Productivity

TFP is estimated using the Solow residual approach to identify the portion of output growth not explained by changes in capital and labour.

The analysis finds substantial variation in TFP growth across the two economies and across different decades.

Broadly:

* **1970s:** Malaysia recorded strong positive TFP growth, while Bangladesh experienced negative TFP growth.
* **1980s–1990s:** Both economies experienced periods of weak or negative TFP growth.
* **2000s:** TFP performance improved in both countries.
* **2010s:** TFP growth became relatively modest in both economies.

These differences suggest that capital accumulation alone cannot fully explain long-run differences in economic performance.

## Human Capital and Institutional Quality

The study also examines human development and institutional conditions as potential explanations for differences in productivity.

Malaysia has generally recorded higher levels of:

* Human development
* Educational attainment
* Average years of schooling
* Institutional quality
* Rule of Law

These factors may improve productivity by increasing worker skills, supporting technological adoption, improving investment conditions, and reducing economic inefficiencies.

The analysis therefore treats human capital and institutions as important **supporting factors for TFP growth**, rather than assuming that they automatically cause higher productivity.

## Convergence Hypothesis

The paper tests the Solow convergence hypothesis by comparing initial GDP per capita in 1970 with subsequent economic growth across 11 Asian economies:

**Bangladesh, Indonesia, Sri Lanka, Philippines, Myanmar, Malaysia, Vietnam, Cambodia, Bhutan, Nepal, and Brunei.**

The analysis investigates whether economies with lower initial income levels experienced faster subsequent growth than relatively richer economies.

The results provide **evidence consistent with conditional convergence**, although the relationship should not be interpreted as proof of unconditional convergence because countries differ in savings, population growth, human capital, technology, and institutional conditions.

## Key Economic Insights

The analysis highlights several broader lessons:

### 1. Capital accumulation is important but insufficient.

Investment in physical capital can increase output, but diminishing returns mean that capital accumulation alone cannot sustain long-run growth indefinitely.

### 2. Productivity is central to long-run growth.

Differences in TFP help explain why economies with similar increases in capital and labour can achieve very different growth outcomes.

### 3. Human capital supports productivity.

Education and skills can improve worker productivity and facilitate the adoption of new technologies.

### 4. Institutions influence economic performance.

Effective institutions can improve investment conditions, resource allocation, and the efficiency with which capital and labour are used.

### 5. Lower-income economies can grow faster.

The convergence analysis provides evidence that poorer Asian economies can achieve relatively rapid growth, although convergence depends on the structural conditions of each economy.
