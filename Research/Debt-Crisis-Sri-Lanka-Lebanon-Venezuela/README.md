# Debt Crisis Analysis: Sri Lanka, Lebanon & Venezuela

## Overview

This research paper examines the development of sovereign debt and broader economic crises in **Sri Lanka, Lebanon, and Venezuela**. The study compares the historical circumstances surrounding each crisis, identifies their major economic and political causes, and evaluates the policy measures and strategies pursued to address them.

Although the three economies experienced crises under very different circumstances, the analysis identifies several recurring factors, including **fiscal and monetary mismanagement, political instability, corruption, dependence on external financing or concentrated sources of revenue, and weaknesses in economic policy and governance**.

The paper concludes by considering the policy lessons that can be drawn from the experiences of the three countries, particularly regarding fiscal discipline, economic diversification, responsible borrowing, investment decisions, and structural reform.

---

## Research Objectives

The paper aims to:

* Examine the historical development of the debt crises in Sri Lanka, Lebanon, and Venezuela.
* Identify the major economic and political factors contributing to each crisis.
* Compare common patterns across the three economies.
* Examine country-specific causes and vulnerabilities.
* Analyse the policy measures adopted to address the crises.
* Consider broader lessons for preventing and managing sovereign debt crises.

## Countries Examined

### Sri Lanka

The paper examines Sri Lanka's accumulation of debt through extensive borrowing for large-scale infrastructure and development projects. It considers the economic consequences of projects that failed to generate their anticipated returns, declining government revenues, political interference, falling investment, and deteriorating foreign-exchange reserves.

The analysis also discusses the resulting shortages of essential goods, inflation, declining tourism and remittances, and difficulties meeting external debt obligations.

**Key issues examined:**

* Infrastructure borrowing
* Public debt accumulation
* Fiscal deficits
* Tax reductions
* Political intervention and corruption
* Foreign-exchange shortages
* Declining investment
* IMF assistance and debt restructuring
* Fiscal consolidation and austerity

---

### Lebanon

The paper examines Lebanon's long-term accumulation of public debt in the context of political instability, sectarian divisions, weak economic growth, and difficulties implementing structural reforms.

Particular attention is given to foreign-currency-denominated debt, exchange-rate pressures, the deterioration of the banking sector, currency depreciation, inflation, declining economic activity, and the consequences of the COVID-19 pandemic and Beirut explosion.

**Key issues examined:**

* High public debt
* Political instability
* Fiscal mismanagement
* Foreign-currency debt
* Exchange-rate pressures
* Banking-sector instability
* Currency depreciation
* Economic contraction
* Economic diversification
* International assistance and structural reform

---

### Venezuela

The paper examines Venezuela's dependence on oil revenues and the vulnerability created by a lack of economic diversification. The decline in global oil prices significantly reduced government revenues, while fiscal deficits and expansionary monetary policies contributed to severe inflation and the erosion of purchasing power.

The study also considers political instability, corruption, capital flight, declining production, international sanctions, and the subsequent attempts to restore economic stability.

**Key issues examined:**

* Oil dependence
* Commodity-price shocks
* Fiscal deficits
* Monetary mismanagement
* Hyperinflation
* Currency depreciation
* Capital flight
* Political instability
* Economic diversification
* Foreign investment and oil-sector recovery

## Key Economic Insights

The analysis highlights several broader lessons from the three cases:

**1. Debt accumulation without sufficient productive returns can become unsustainable.**
Borrowing for development projects does not necessarily generate sustainable growth if projects fail to produce sufficient economic returns.

**2. Economic diversification can reduce vulnerability.**
Venezuela's dependence on oil illustrates the risks associated with relying heavily on a single source of export earnings and government revenue.

**3. Fiscal and monetary policies are closely interconnected.**
Persistent fiscal deficits can create severe economic instability when governments rely on unsustainable borrowing or monetary financing.

**4. Governance matters for economic stability.**
Political instability, corruption, and poor allocation of public resources can undermine otherwise potentially productive economic policies.

**5. External shocks can amplify existing weaknesses.**
Events such as commodity-price changes, the COVID-19 pandemic, geopolitical developments, and declining investor confidence can become significantly more damaging when an economy already has substantial structural vulnerabilities.
